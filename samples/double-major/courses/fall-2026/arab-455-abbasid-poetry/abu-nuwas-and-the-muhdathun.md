## who the muḥdathūn are

**muḥdaṯūn** (مُحْدَثُون, "those made new", i.e. the moderns): the poets of the ʿAbbāsid period from roughly the revolution onward, as against the **qudamāʾ** (the ancients, jāhilī and early Islamic). The label is the critics', and at first a reproach: new = without precedent, and precedent is authority. By the late 3rd/9th century it is a neutral period term, and by Ibn al-Muʿtazz partly a compliment.

the "moderns", the ʿAbbāsid-era poets set against the ancients (qudamāʾ) by the critics, are the {{c1::muḥdaṯūn (مُحْدَثُون)}} ^ab-muhdathun

What is new, from the board in wk 4:

1. **audience**: the court and the city (Baghdad from 145/762, Basra, Kufa) instead of the tribe; a literate class of kuttāb (secretaries) who read poetry as well as heard it.
2. **poets**: many of non-Arab descent (mawālī), esp. Persian. Baššār boasts of it. The šuʿūbiyya controversy is in the background [Prof. Haddad says we will not do šuʿūbiyya properly, read the EI article if I want it].
3. **genres** separated out of the qaṣīda as independent short poems: the wine poem, the hunt poem, the love lyric (ġazal), the ascetic poem. The qaṣīda remains for madīḥ.
4. **style**: badīʿ (below), which is the real fight.
5. **the nasīb** becomes a site of play: kept, varied, parodied.

## Abū Nuwās

al-Ḥasan b. Hāniʾ al-Ḥakamī, Abū Nuwās. Born in Ahvaz (al-Ahwāz, Ḫūzistān) somewhere in the 130s–140s/750s, Persian mother, Arab father of the Ḥakam (South Arabian clientage claimed). Grew up in Basra. Trained with the poet Wāliba b. al-Ḥubāb and the philologist-poet Ḫalaf al-Aḥmar, who (story) made him memorize a thousand ancient poems and then forget them before he would let him compose. Prof. Haddad: "that anecdote is a theory of originality, and a better one than most."

Went to Baghdad, attached to the Barmakids and Hārūn al-Rašīd's court (r. 170–193/786–809), then the boon companion (nadīm) of al-Amīn (r. 193–198/809–813). Died around the time of the civil war, c. 198/813 (sources vary to 200/815). The dīwān was collected after his death by different editors (Ḥamza al-Iṣfahānī, al-Ṣūlī), and the recensions differ a lot, which matters for attribution: lots of wine poetry gets attributed to him because it sounds like him.

Genres he works in, as the dīwān is organized (by ġaraḍ, which is itself significant: the editors broke the qaṣīda into themes):

- **khamriyyāt** (wine poems): the core of his reputation
- **ṭardiyyāt** (hunt poems): he is the major early practitioner; the hound, the falcon, often in rajaz
- **ġazal**: mudhakkar (on boys) and muʾannaṯ (on women); the Ǧinān poems
- **madīḥ** for the caliphs and the Barmakids, mostly conventional, some brilliant
- **hijāʾ**, **ʿitāb** (reproach), **zuhdiyyāt** (late ascetic poems; whether they are a conversion or a genre exercise is a whole debate)
- **mujūn**: the obscene/libertine; overlaps with all of the above

## the khamriyya

Wine poetry existed before him: jāhilī poets boast of drinking in the faḫr (Ṭarafa, al-Aʿšā), and in the Umayyad period al-Walīd b. Yazīd wrote independent wine poems that Abū Nuwās drew on. What he did was make the wine poem a full genre with its own conventions and its own argument with the qaṣīda.

خَمْرِيَّة ::: khamriyya — the wine poem; as an independent genre, chiefly Abū Nuwās's ^ab-khamriyya
<!-- sr due=2026-11-19 st=review s=27.9 d=3.7 r=4 l=0 ivl=28 last=2026-10-22T08:35:00Z g=3 -->

The conventions (we built this list in wk 5 from four poems):

- the night visit: the speaker and companions wake the vintner, knocking at the tavern after dark. The vintner is often a Christian, Jew, or Zoroastrian, so the tavern is the edge of the Muslim city: monasteries (dayr), Christian feast days.
- the wine personified: a virgin kept sealed in the jar (dann) since the time of ʿĀd or Noah; deflowered when the seal is broken. Its age outdoes the antiquity of the ruins. Its light lights the room, it is "sun", "lamp", "fire that Zoroastrians would worship".
- the mixing with water: bubbles as pearls, the cup as a body.
- the cupbearer (sāqī), the beloved figure who replaces the departing woman of the nasīb.
- the censor (ʿāḏil, lāʾim) who blames the drinker, and whom the drinker answers: a convention from the old nasīb (the companions who tell you to be patient) turned around.
- sin and forgiveness: God's mercy is greater than the sin, so the pious who refuse it are presumptuous. Theological play, sometimes Muʿtazilī-flavored.

The famous opening (wk 5, Prof. Haddad had us all read it aloud):

> دَعْ عَنْكَ لَوْمِي فَإِنَّ اللَّوْمَ إِغْرَاءُ / وَدَاوِنِي بِالَّتِي كَانَتْ هِيَ الدَّاءُ

"Leave off blaming me, for blame is an incitement / and cure me with the very thing that was the disease." Meter basīṭ, rhyme in hamza (a hamziyya). The address to the censor, the medical paradox (the hair of the dog), the theological undertone: lawm is what the ʿāḏil does, and blame produces the sin it forbids.

دَعْ عَنْكَ لَوْمِي فَإِنَّ اللَّوْمَ إِغْرَاءُ / {{c1::وَدَاوِنِي بِالَّتِي كَانَتْ هِيَ الدَّاءُ}} ^ab-da-anka

## the mock-nasīb

This is what the wk 5 discussion was really about. Abū Nuwās repeatedly makes fun of the ruin-prelude: why stand weeping at a campsite you never lived in, in a desert you have never seen, when there is a tavern in town. Two lines everyone cites:

> قُلْ لِمَنْ يَبْكِي عَلَى رَسْمٍ دَرَسْ / وَاقِفًا مَا ضَرَّ لَوْ كَانَ جَلَسْ

"Say to the one who weeps over an effaced trace, / standing: what harm if he sat down?" Meter ramal (fāʿilātun fāʿilātun fāʿilun). The joke is literal: wuqūf (halting, standing) is the convention; he proposes sitting. Sitting = the drinking session (maǧlis, from the same root as jalasa). So the whole nasīb tradition is being asked to sit down and have a drink.

> عَاجَ الشَّقِيُّ عَلَى رَسْمٍ يُسَائِلُهُ / وَعُجْتُ أَسْأَلُ عَنْ خَمَّارَةِ البَلَدِ

"The wretch turned aside to an abode, to question it, / and I turned aside to ask after the town's wine-shop." Same verb (ʿāja / ʿujtu, to turn the mount aside), same act of asking, opposite object. The parody needs the convention to work, which is Prof. Haddad's point: the mock-nasīb is a nasīb.

قُلْ لِمَنْ يَبْكِي عَلَى رَسْمٍ دَرَسْ / {{c1::وَاقِفًا مَا ضَرَّ لَوْ كَانَ جَلَسْ}} (Abū Nuwās, meter {{c2::ramal}}) ^ab-qul-li-man

Three readings of the mock-nasīb from the session:

1. **cultural**: an urban, partly Persian, secretarial culture rejecting Bedouin nostalgia. The šuʿūbī reading. Prof. Haddad thinks it is too simple: Abū Nuwās also wrote straight nasībs and was proud of his command of Bedouin diction.
2. **generic**: the wine poem defines itself by inverting the qaṣīda's opening; it is a counter-qaṣīda (Kennedy's line, roughly, as she presented it). The tavern is the new aṭlāl, the vintner the new interlocutor, the jar the new relic of antiquity.
3. **critical**: the poems are a position in the quarrel between philologists and moderns. Ibn Qutayba's warning against the poet who stops at a mud-brick ruin (see the qaṣīda tab) answers exactly this.

Kamila: (2) and (3) are the same thing. Prof. Haddad: no, (2) is about the poem's form and (3) about its audience; a parody can be formally serious and socially frivolous.

My note to self for the paper: al-Mutanabbī comes a century and a half later and does the *opposite*: he keeps the old diction, raises it, and makes the qaṣīda about himself. The muḥdaṯ game of playing with the convention is over; he treats the convention as a stage. [use in intro? maybe too broad]

## wk 6: badīʿ and the quarrel

**badīʿ** (بَدِيع, "new, novel, original"): the use of rhetorical figures, esp. metaphor (istiʿāra), paronomasia (tajnīs / jinās) and antithesis (ṭibāq / muṭābaqa), densely and conspicuously. The moderns did not invent the figures; they made them the point. Muslim b. al-Walīd is credited with the first deliberate program; Abū Tammām takes it so far that his poems needed commentaries in his own lifetime.

The critical quarrel:

- The philologists (Ibn al-Aʿrābī, d. 231/846, is the famous hostile one) judged poetry by ancient usage; a metaphor with no precedent was a mistake. There is a story that Ibn al-Aʿrābī admired some verses until he heard they were Abū Tammām's, then had them struck out. [check: which source? Prof. Haddad told it, I didn't get the reference]
- **Ibn al-Muʿtazz**, *Kitāb al-Badīʿ* (274/887): argues that badīʿ is found in the Qurʾān, the ḥadīṯ, and the ancients; the moderns only used it more, and the excess is the fault (he does not much like Abū Tammām either). He gives **five figures** of badīʿ proper: istiʿāra (metaphor), tajnīs (paronomasia), muṭābaqa (antithesis), radd aʿjāz al-kalām ʿalā mā taqaddamahā (repeating at the end of a line a word from earlier in it), al-maḏhab al-kalāmī (argument in the manner of the theologians). Then twelve or so "beauties" (maḥāsin) of speech.
- **al-Āmidī** (d. 370/980 or so), *al-Muwāzana bayna al-Ṭāʾiyyayn*: the "weighing" of Abū Tammām against al-Buḥturī (both of the tribe of Ṭayyiʾ, hence "the two Ṭāʾīs"). Al-Buḥturī = maṭbūʿ, natural; Abū Tammām = maṣnūʿ, crafted, "the poet of the ṣanʿa". Al-Āmidī prefers al-Buḥturī without quite saying so.

### the Amorium ode

Abū Tammām's bāʾiyya on the caliph al-Muʿtaṣim's capture of Amorium (ʿAmmūriya) in Byzantine Anatolia, 223/838. Background from the poem itself: the astrologers had said the city could not be taken before the figs and grapes ripened; the caliph marched anyway and took it. The opening:

> السَّيْفُ أَصْدَقُ أَنْبَاءً مِنَ الكُتُبِ / فِي حَدِّهِ الحَدُّ بَيْنَ الجِدِّ وَاللَّعِبِ

"The sword is truer in its tidings than the books: / in its edge is the boundary between earnest and play." Basīṭ, rhyme in bāʾ.

The badīʿ in one line: tajnīs tāmm in ḥadd (edge) / ḥadd (boundary), near-tajnīs between those and jidd (earnestness); ṭibāq between jidd and laʿib. And the argument: the "books" are the astrologers' books, so the line is also a polemic against astrology in favor of the caliph's action, i.e. the madīḥ's claim (legitimacy proven by victory) in the opening line instead of after a nasīb. No nasīb at all: he opens on the ġaraḍ. Prof. Haddad: this is one of the places where the court ode tells you it can do without the prelude when the occasion is big enough. S. Stetkevych reads the whole poem as a ritual of sacrifice and victory [Abū Tammām book, ch. on the ʿAmmūriya ode].

Note to self on Abū Tammām: the Ḥamāsa (his anthology of older poetry, arranged by theme, ḥamāsa = valor being the first chapter) shows that the most extreme modern was also a great connoisseur of the ancients. Same pattern as Abū Nuwās and Ḫalaf: you must own the tradition to break it. al-Mutanabbī, again, is the endpoint: owns it, does not break it, bends it around himself.

## what Prof. Haddad wants us to carry forward

Written at the end of wk 6, verbatim as best I could:

"Three things. The qaṣīda survives the moderns, in the court, for madīḥ. The moderns change what a poet is: a professional with a style, judged against other named poets, not a tribe's voice. And criticism is now part of poetry: from here on a poet writes knowing the critics will weigh him. Al-Mutanabbī is the first poet who writes as if he has already read his own reviews."
