## life, in order

Abū l-Ṭayyib Aḥmad b. al-Ḥusayn al-Juʿfī, called al-Mutanabbī. Born **303/915** in Kufa, in the Kinda quarter. Hostile sources make his father a water-carrier (saqqāʾ); he himself claims noble South Arabian descent (Juʿfī), and keeps quiet about his father in the poems, which the critics noticed. Prof. Haddad: "the silence about the father is one of the loudest things in the dīwān." [for the paper: the self-made lineage. Larkin ch. 1 has this.]

- **312/924**: the Qarāmiṭa sack Kufa; the family goes into the desert (bādiya) for a time. Tradition says he learned pure Bedouin Arabic there. True or not, it is the story he needed: the city boy with the Bedouin tongue.
- **school in Kufa, then Baghdad briefly, then Syria** from c. 321/933, writing panegyrics for minor patrons for small rewards. The early poems already full of himself.
- **c. 322–324/933–935**: some kind of rising among the Bedouin of the Samāwa (Banū Kalb), with a prophetic or messianic claim, or a political one that his enemies made prophetic. Arrested by Luʾluʾ, the governor of Ḥimṣ, imprisoned about two years. Hence the laqab: al-Mutanabbī, "the one who claims to be a prophet". Another explanation (his own, later?) is that the name comes from a line comparing himself to a prophet among an ungrateful people. Prof. Haddad: both may be true and the name stuck because it fit the voice.
- **328/939–40**: Badr b. ʿAmmār in Tiberias, the first real patron.
- **337/948 – 346/957**: **Sayf al-Dawla** in Aleppo. Nine years, the core of the dīwān: the Sayfiyyāt.
- **346/957 – 350/962**: Egypt, **Kāfūr** al-Ixšīdī. The Kāfūriyyāt and then the satires.
- **350/962**: flees Egypt on the eve of ʿĪd al-Aḍḥā.
- then Kufa, Baghdad (refuses to praise the Būyid vizier al-Muhallabī; the run-in with al-Ḥātimī), Ibn al-ʿAmīd at Arraǧān, **ʿAḍud al-Dawla** at Shiraz (354/965).
- **354/965**, Ramaḍān: killed on the road back to Iraq near Dayr al-ʿĀqūl, by Fātik b. Abī Jahl al-Asadī, kinsman of a man (Ḍabba) he had satirized obscenely. With him his son Muḥassad and a slave.

## Sayf al-Dawla (wk 7)

ʿAlī b. ʿAbd Allāh b. Ḥamdān, Sayf al-Dawla ("Sword of the State", a title from the caliph), Ḥamdānid emir of Aleppo from 333/944 to his death in 356/967. A frontier ruler: years of campaigns against the Byzantines (the Phokas family, Bardas then Nikephoros), with victories and serious defeats. His court was the great literary court of its time: al-Fārābī, the grammarian Ibn Ḫālawayh, his cousin the poet Abū Firās, the preacher Ibn Nubāta, and al-Mutanabbī.

What is unusual about the relationship (seminar list):

1. **the contract**: by the sources he would not recite standing or kiss the ground, and recited seated; whether or not literally true, the poems themselves present the poet as near-equal. He praises the emir and himself in the same breath.
2. **the battle odes** are reportage as well as praise: specific places, specific campaigns. The emir is praised as the one who acts; the poet as the one who knows and says what the act means.
3. **the rivals**: Abū Firās (a Ḥamdānid, a prince, and a poet, so the one competitor al-Mutanabbī could not condescend to), Ibn Ḫālawayh (story: struck him in the face with a key / an inkwell during an argument in the emir's presence, and the emir did nothing). The final poems are about being undervalued at court.
4. **the break**: the reproach-mīmiyya (below, "wā ḥarra qalbāh") and departure in 346/957.

### the al-Ḥadaṯ ode (343/954)

On Sayf al-Dawla's rebuilding and defense of the frontier fortress of al-Ḥadaṯ (al-Ḥadaṯ al-Ḥamrāʾ) against the Byzantine army. Opens without a nasīb, on a maxim (ḥikma), meter ṭawīl, rhyme in mīm:

> عَلَى قَدْرِ أَهْلِ العَزْمِ تَأْتِي العَزَائِمُ / وَتَأْتِي عَلَى قَدْرِ الكِرَامِ المَكَارِمُ

"Resolves come in the measure of the people of resolve, / and noble deeds come in the measure of the noble."

The figures: radd al-ʿajuz ʿalā al-ṣadr in the strong sense (ʿazm / ʿazāʾim, kirām / makārim), and the chiasmus of the two hemistichs: X comes in the measure of Y, then comes in the measure of Y, X. The line is a definition of madīḥ: the praise (and the deed) is proportioned to the man. Then the next line inverts it for the small: the small think small things big, the great think great things small. So from line 1, the poem is about measure, which means it is about who has the right to measure. Who? The poet.

Prof. Haddad's reading, which I want for the paper: the ode stages a double heroism. The emir builds the fortress and holds it; the poet builds the poem and holds the meaning. "The ḥikma opening takes the place of the nasīb and does its work: it tells you what the poem knows before the poem has said anything about the patron."

### the reproach: "wā ḥarra qalbāh"

The mīmiyya beginning *wā ḥarra qalbāh* ("oh the burning of my heart") is a complaint to Sayf al-Dawla: the poet loves the emir, the emir does not love him back, the court (the envious, the slanderers) has come between them. It is ʿitāb, reproach, which is a genre with rules: you can blame a patron for neglect as long as the blame is a form of love. Al-Mutanabbī uses the form to say things no court poet says. In it, the two most famous self-praise lines in Arabic, both basīṭ:

> أَنَا الَّذِي نَظَرَ الأَعْمَى إِلَى أَدَبِي / وَأَسْمَعَتْ كَلِمَاتِي مَنْ بِهِ صَمَمُ

"I am the one whose literary art the blind have seen, / and my words have made the deaf hear."

> الخَيْلُ وَاللَّيْلُ وَالبَيْدَاءُ تَعْرِفُنِي / وَالسَّيْفُ وَالرُّمْحُ وَالقِرْطَاسُ وَالقَلَمُ

"The horses and the night and the desert know me, / and the sword and the spear and the paper and the pen."

Seminar on the second line: a catalogue in which the first hemistich is the raḥīl (horses, night, desert) and the second hemistich is the faḫr of the warrior (sword, spear) turning, at the last two words, into the poet's tools (paper, pen). The tribal hero and the professional writer in one line, the writer last, having the rhyme. Yusuf: the qalam is the rhyme word because it is the last word, i.e. it has the final say. Prof. Haddad: "and the patron's name is nowhere in this line, in a poem addressed to the patron."

## Egypt and Kāfūr (wk 8)

Kāfūr, Abū l-Misk, a Black eunuch of East African origin, enslaved and bought by Muḥammad b. Ṭuġj al-Ixšīd, the founder of the Ixšīdid dynasty in Egypt; rose to command and became regent for the Ixšīd's young sons, and from 355/966 ruler in his own name until his death in 357/968. By every account a capable ruler.

Al-Mutanabbī came to Fusṭāṭ in 346/957 from Aleppo, by way of Damascus. What he wanted, by his own poems: a governorship (wilāya), a province or a city (Ṣaydā is mentioned in the sources). He did not get it. Kāfūr gave money and kept him close, but never office; the sources say Kāfūr feared a man who had claimed prophethood would claim a throne next. About four years, then the flight.

### the Kāfūriyyāt

The panegyrics to Kāfūr, about ten long poems. The critics from early on read them as double: lines that praise in one reading and insult in another. Prof. Haddad's handout had examples where the praise of Kāfūr's blackness, or of his being self-made, can be heard as mockery. Seminar argument, which went long:

- **Reading A (the medieval one)**: he was always contemptuous; the panegyrics are covert satire, ironized from the start. Supported by the fact that he satirized Kāfūr viciously afterwards, so the medieval commentators read the satire back into the praise.
- **Reading B (Larkin, as presented; also Prof. Haddad partly)**: the panegyrics are real panegyrics, under real strain. The ambiguity is the strain of praising a man the poet cannot praise in his usual mode (as warrior, as noble by lineage), which forces him into a different praise (the man who made himself, the ruler by merit). The poet sees himself in Kāfūr and hates it. [paper: this is my hook]
- **Kamila**: a third possibility, the ambiguity is a hedge, written so that it can be read either way later, depending on how things go. A poem as an option.
- Me: the hedge reading and Reading B are compatible: the strain is what makes the hedge possible. Prof. Haddad: "then the paper has to show it in the lines, not in the biography."

### the flight and the dāliyya

He fled Fusṭāṭ in secret on the eve of the ʿĪd al-Aḍḥā, 350/962 (it was January 962). On the road, the satire of Kāfūr in dāl, which opens:

> عِيدٌ بِأَيَّةِ حَالٍ عُدْتَ يَا عِيدُ / بِمَا مَضَى أَمْ بِأَمْرٍ فِيكَ تَجْدِيدُ

"Feast-day, in what state have you returned, feast-day? / With what is past, or with some matter new in you?" Basīṭ. The pun in ʿīd / ʿudta (the feast is the thing that returns, from ʿ-w-d) and the question: is this a return of the same, or a renewal? It is a nasīb-substitute: the poet alone on a feast day, far from the beloved, who is now glory itself.

The poem then becomes the most savage satire in the dīwān, and its attack is racial: on Kāfūr's blackness and his enslaved origin, in terms that are flatly racist. The best-known line of it is a maxim about never buying a slave without a stick. Prof. Haddad spent twenty minutes on this. Her points: (1) the poem is famous, endlessly anthologized, and its proverb lines have been used as proverbs, and we cannot read the Kāfūriyyāt honestly without reading it; (2) the racism is not incidental decoration: it is the poem's argument, that Kāfūr's rule over free Arabs is unnatural, and the panegyrics' praise of the self-made ruler is withdrawn by denying that a slave can be made into anything; (3) the poem is also a satire of Egypt and its people, who "let" this happen, and of the poet himself for having come. I am not quoting the stick line in the paper beyond identifying it. [check with Prof. Haddad whether she wants the Arabic quoted in full when we discuss it; she said "quote what you analyze"]

What the Kāfūr cycle does to the voice, notes from the end of wk 8:

- the Sayfiyyāt: poet and patron as a pair of heroes; the self-praise is borne by the patron's greatness (praising a great man makes a great poet).
- the Kāfūriyyāt: the patron cannot bear the poet's self-image, so the self-praise detaches. The poems turn inward: complaint, the world's injustice, maxims about time (dahr). Many of the famous ḥikma lines on fortune and betrayal are from the Egyptian years.
- the satires: the poet takes back the gift. If the madīḥ is an exchange (Stetkevych), the hijāʾ after a madīḥ is the return of the counterfeit coin.

## style (wk 9, partly)

What the critics praised and blamed, as a list (filled out from the handout):

- **ḥikma**: aphoristic lines on fortune, death, ambition, the low and the noble, phrased to be detachable. Many became proverbs (amṯāl). The wāfir line *iḏā ġāmarta fī šarafin marūmi / fa-lā taqnaʿ bi-mā dūna l-nujūmi* is one.
- **faḫr in madīḥ**: the poet's self as the measure of the praise.
- **mubālaġa** (hyperbole) pushed past the limits critics accepted; sometimes approaching the theologically risky (praising a patron in terms that belong to God or a prophet).
- **diction**: the old Bedouin word-stock (ġarīb) used deliberately, and grammatical liberties (unusual plurals, contested constructions) that the grammarians attacked and his defenders (Ibn Jinnī) defended.
- **the opening**: often not the classical nasīb; complaint, maxim, or a direct address. When he does write a nasīb it is short and turns quickly to himself.
- **muḥdaṯ figures** used, but without the Abū Tammām density; he is "between the two Ṭāʾīs".

## the critics (wk 9)

Never had a poet so many enemies with pens:

- **al-Ṣāḥib b. ʿAbbād** (d. 385/995), Būyid vizier, whom al-Mutanabbī refused to praise: *al-Kašf ʿan masāwiʾ al-Mutanabbī* ("Exposing al-Mutanabbī's faults").
- **al-Ḥātimī**: the *Risāla al-mūḍiḥa*, an account (self-serving) of a debate with al-Mutanabbī in Baghdad, accusing him of stealing his meanings (sariqāt) from earlier poets.
- **al-Qāḍī al-Jurjānī** (ʿAlī b. ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz, d. 392/1002): *al-Wasāṭa bayna al-Mutanabbī wa-ḫuṣūmihi* ("Mediation between al-Mutanabbī and his adversaries"). Balanced: every great poet has faults, so the question is the proportion; theory of sariqa (when borrowing a meaning is legitimate: if you improve it).
- **commentators**: Ibn Jinnī (d. 392/1002, the grammarian, a friend: *al-Fasr*), al-Maʿarrī (*Muʿjiz Aḥmad*), al-Wāḥidī (d. 468/1076). The volume of commentary is itself the evidence of his status.
