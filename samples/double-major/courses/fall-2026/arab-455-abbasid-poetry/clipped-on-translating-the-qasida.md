Every translator of classical Arabic poetry eventually meets the same wall, and it is not vocabulary. Vocabulary can be looked up; the commentators were glossing the hard words of the old odes a thousand years ago, and their glosses are still the best dictionary we have. The wall is the shape of the poem. A qaṣīda is a single long breath held across fifty or eighty lines, each line split in two, every line ending on the same consonant and the same vowel. English has nothing like it, and every choice the translator makes is a choice about which part of that shape to give up.

Begin with the rhyme. A monorhyme over sixty lines is not a strain in Arabic, which builds words from roots and patterns and so produces rhymes the way a loom produces cloth. In English it is a stunt. The translator who keeps it ends up choosing words for their endings, and the reader hears the strain in every line. The translator who drops it loses something the original listener never stopped hearing: the return, line after line, to one sound, which is how the poem tells you it is still one poem while it wanders from the ruined camp to the desert to the patron's hall.

So most modern translators drop it, and try to give the unity back in some other way. Some keep a single vowel at line-ends without full rhyme. Some keep the rhyme only in the opening line, as the Arabic poets themselves marked the first line by rhyming both its halves. Some give up on sound entirely and lean on the line: a strong, end-stopped English line, each one complete, so that the reader feels the bayt as a unit even without its music.

That last choice is closer to the poem than it may look. The classical critics praised a line that stood on its own and blamed one whose sense ran over into the next. The qaṣīda is built of complete lines the way a wall is built of stones, and the joints show. An English translation that runs the sense on from line to line, in the manner of blank verse, makes the poem read more smoothly and less like itself.

Then there are the names. The ruins in the old preludes are always somewhere: between this hill and that dune, at the edge of a sand-tract with a name. To the poem's first audience these were places some of them knew. To an ʿAbbāsid audience in Baghdad they were already words from an old map, and their strangeness was part of their weight. To an English reader they are nothing at all unless the translator does something. Leave them as transliterations and the line clots with italics. Translate them ("the Bend of the Sands", "the Two Wells") and the poem acquires a fairy-tale geography the Arabic never had. Drop them and you lose the thing the prelude is about, which is that the place was real and is gone.

My own compromise, which pleases nobody, is to keep the names where the line can bear them and to trust the reader to hear in a strange name what the ʿAbbāsid listener heard: a place that exists now only because a poem said it.

Consider the most famous first line in the language. Literally: "Halt, you two, let us weep at the memory of a beloved and an abode, at the sand's edge between al-Dakhūl and Ḥawmal." That is accurate and dead. One can try for life: "Stop, both of you. Let us weep for her and for the place, where the dune runs out between Dakhul and Hawmal." Better, perhaps, and already wrong: the Arabic does not say "her", and the memory is the subject, not the woman. Every gain of voice is a loss of something the commentators would have defended.

The address to two companions is a small case of the whole problem. The verb is dual: the poet speaks to two people. Grammarians argued about why. The convention lasted long after anyone imagined real companions at the ruins; later poets address the pair as a matter of form. English has no dual, and "you two" sounds either folksy or legal. Yet the dual matters. It makes the prelude a scene with witnesses rather than a private lament. The poet weeps in company, and the companions, later in many odes, tell him to stop. Lose the two and you lose the first audience inside the poem, the one that stands for us.

The court odes of the ʿAbbāsid poets add a further difficulty, which is praise. Modern English readers are embarrassed by panegyric. We hear flattery, and we translate it as if apologizing: softening the hyperbole, explaining it in footnotes, or choosing the poems that praise least. But praise in these poems is not a lapse of taste. It is the work the poem was commissioned to do, and its excess is a craft. When a poet says that a ruler's generosity shames the rain, he is not making a meteorological claim; he is entering a contest with every earlier poet who praised a ruler's generosity, and the audience is scoring him. To translate the hyperbole timidly is to make the poet lose a contest he won.

The best of the court poets also knew how to make praise carry other freight: self-praise, complaint, a claim on the patron, sometimes a warning. A translator who flattens the praise flattens all of this with it. The great panegyrists are often at their most personal exactly where they are most formal, and the formal surface has to be kept for the personal turn to be felt.

What is left? Perhaps only this: a translation of a qaṣīda should let the reader feel that it is one of many. The poem was made inside a tradition so dense that every image answered another image, every line was weighed against a line some earlier poet had made. The reader of a translation sees one poem alone, like a single tile pried from a wall. The translator can at least leave the edges rough, so that the reader can see it was once fitted to others.

I have come, after many years, to prefer translations that admit they are translations: that keep the line and its break, keep some names, keep the two companions even when English stumbles over them, and print the Arabic on the facing page so that the reader who cannot read it can at least see its shape, the two columns of hemistichs running down the page with their single rhyme letter standing at the end of each line like a row of posts. The shape is the first thing the poem says.
