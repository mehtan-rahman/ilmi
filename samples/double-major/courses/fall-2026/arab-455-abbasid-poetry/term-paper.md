## §1 introduction (rough)

The most famous line al-Mutanabbī wrote is a list:

> الخَيْلُ وَاللَّيْلُ وَالبَيْدَاءُ تَعْرِفُنِي / وَالسَّيْفُ وَالرُّمْحُ وَالقِرْطَاسُ وَالقَلَمُ

The horses and the night and the desert know me, / and the sword and the spear and the paper and the pen.

It comes from a poem addressed to his patron, Sayf al-Dawla, the Ḥamdānid emir of Aleppo, and it does not mention him. Its first hemistich is the old raḥīl compressed into three nouns, the camel-journey through the night desert that brought the pre-Islamic poet to his tribe or his patron; its second hemistich is the faḫr of the warrior, turning in its last two words into the tools of the professional poet. The line moves through the whole history of the qaṣīda and ends on the pen, which has the rhyme. In a panegyric it is the poet who is known, and the things that know him are not the court. [this paragraph is ok. maybe too pleased with itself]

The line is usually read as the purest expression of al-Mutanabbī's self-regard, and so it is. But it is also an argument about the panegyric, made from inside one. The court ode, as the ʿAbbāsid poets inherited it, was an exchange: the poet brought his praise to the patron's door, having suffered the road to get there, and the patron was bound to return a gift. [cite Stetkevych 2002 here, the exchange model; and Ibn Qutayba's account as the medieval statement of the same thing] What the line claims is that the poet brings something besides praise. He brings himself, and the self is already known: by the desert, by the night, by the pen. The patron receives a poet whose worth is established elsewhere.

This paper follows that claim through three moments of al-Mutanabbī's career: [list the three poems once chosen] I argue that ...

What I am not doing: the biography (Blachère, Larkin) except where the poems require it; and the old question of whether the panegyrics to Kāfūr are covert satire, which I take as a symptom rather than a question to settle. ## §2 Aleppo: the double hero

The ode on al-Ḥadaṯ (343/954) has no nasīb. It opens on a maxim:

> عَلَى قَدْرِ أَهْلِ العَزْمِ تَأْتِي العَزَائِمُ / وَتَأْتِي عَلَى قَدْرِ الكِرَامِ المَكَارِمُ

Resolves come in the measure of the people of resolve, / and noble deeds come in the measure of the noble.

The line is built as a chiasmus: resolve and the resolute in the first half, the noble and noble deeds in the second, with *taʾtī* "come" at the hinge, so the second hemistich reverses the order of the first around the same verb. Each half ends on a broken plural of the word that began it (ʿazm → ʿazāʾim, karam → makārim), so the rhyme word is the abstract quality multiplied: the many deeds that come from the one disposition. [check: is ʿazāʾim plural of ʿazm or of ʿazīma? Wehr: ʿazīma pl. ʿazāʾim. So the line goes ʿazm (resolution, disposition) → ʿazāʾim (resolves, plural of ʿazīma, individual acts of resolution). Rewrite this sentence. It still works: the one disposition and its many acts.]

What the maxim does in the place of the nasīb is announce the poem's subject as measure (qadr). Deeds are proportioned to their doers. The second line makes the consequence explicit: in the eye of the small, small things look great; in the eye of the great, great things look small. A poem about a battle has begun with a theory of perception, and the question it leaves open is whose eye is the true one.

The emir's eye, first: the fortress that the Byzantines thought impregnable is, to him, a small thing. But the poem's eye is the poet's, and the first word of the ode is a preposition of measure, ʿalā qadri. The poet measures the emir before he has named him. [this is the key move of §2, make it slower]

[next: the battle narrative in the middle of the poem. pick 3–4 lines. The ones about the Domestikos fleeing, Prof. Haddad's handout ll. 30ff. #TODO: get line numbers from al-Barqūqī.]

The relationship this sets up is one I will call the double hero [is there a better term? Hamori probably has one, check *The Composition of Mutanabbī's Panegyrics*]. The emir acts; the poet knows what the act means. The praise is real (nobody reading the battle passage thinks the poet is holding back) but it is praise that requires a praiser of matching size. The poem's logic is that only a great poet could see how great the emir is, because in the eye of the small, great things look small. So the madīḥ is also faḫr by entailment.

[this is where the exchange model comes in: the patron's return is the poet's recognition. Sayf al-Dawla pays for the poem; the poem pays for the poet. If the return is withheld, the poet's half of the double hero has nothing to stand on. -> §3]

## §3 Aleppo, ending: the reproach

Notes only.

- ʿitāb as a genre with rules: the blame must be a form of love. The poem starts *wā ḥarra qalbāh*, the burning of the heart, i.e. the nasīb's language of love-pain now aimed at the patron. Sayf al-Dawla is the beloved who has turned away.
- the envious at court = the censors (ʿuḏḏāl) of the nasīb? check whether that is overreading.
- the self-praise lines (anā lladhī naẓara l-aʿmā, al-ḫaylu wa-l-laylu) in this poem are a *demand*: the return owed. "Know what you have." The line about the horses and the pen = the poet's credentials presented for payment.
- he leaves within the year. The exchange did not break dramatically; it was withdrawn.

[Kamila's question in wk 9: is the reproach-poem the sign that the exchange already failed, or the last attempt to keep it? I think: both, and that is why it is in the paper. The poem works as long as Sayf al-Dawla might still answer.]

## §5 the dāliyya

- opening: ʿīdun bi-ayyati ḥālin ʿudta yā ʿīdu. ʿĪd / ʿudta: the feast is what returns. The question to the feast day = the question of whether the exchange can return (renewal) or only repeat (what is past).
- addressed to the feast day. No patron, no companions. (See the extract on the two companions under the clipped article.)
- the satire: the gift taken back. The panegyrics praised Kāfūr as a man made by merit; the satire denies that an enslaved man can be made into anything. That is the racism and it is the argument. I have to say this plainly: the poem's "liberation" of the voice is a liberation into contempt. Not a triumph.
