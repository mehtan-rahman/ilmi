## Larkin, *Al-Mutanabbi* (2008), chs. 1–3

Read 10-05 to 10-20. The best single book for the paper; short, readable, and the persona argument is close to mine (which is a problem: I need to say what I add).

- ch. 1 (life): sensible about the sources; treats the prophethood episode as murky and the name as stuck. The father: notes al-Mutanabbī never names him in the dīwān. [This is where Prof. Haddad's "loudest silence" comes from? or her own? Ask.]
- ch. 2 (Aleppo): the relationship with Sayf al-Dawla as unusually close and unusually tense. The battle odes as both reportage and myth. Good on the al-Ḥadaṯ ode's structure.
- ch. 3 (Egypt): the Kāfūriyyāt read as real panegyrics under strain; the ambiguity as the product of the situation rather than a secret plan. Treats the dāliyya's racism directly, does not soften it. [my Reading B in the seminar notes is from here; check that I am not overstating her]
- "voice" in the title = the poet's persona as the unifying thing in the dīwān. Her "ʿAbbasid poetic ideal": al-Mutanabbī as the culmination of the tradition, not a break from it.

What I add (attempt): the exchange model. Larkin is about the persona; Stetkevych is about the exchange; I want the persona *as what is exchanged*, and what happens to it when the exchange fails. [say this in the presentation in one sentence]

## S. Stetkevych, *The Poetics of Islamic Legitimacy* (2002), intro + al-Mutanabbī chapter

Read 10-12 to 10-26, intro twice.

- Intro: the panegyric qaṣīda as a ritual exchange, drawing on Mauss: the ode is a gift, the patron's reward a counter-gift, the exchange constitutes the patron's legitimacy and binds both parties. The ode also preserves the record of the exchange, so it outlasts the occasion.
- The sacrifice/supplication structure carried over from the tribal ode: the poet's suffering on the road as the claim.
- Her al-Mutanabbī chapter [check: is it on the al-Ḥadaṯ ode? I think so, the battle and the fortress as ritual. Pages?]: reads the battle as a ritual of renewal, the emir as the restorer of order, the poem as the ceremony that makes the victory legitimate.
- What I take: the exchange; the poem as a record that can be revoked. What I leave: the myth-ritual apparatus beyond that (the paper would turn into a methods paper).
- Objection I need to answer: she reads the court ode as primarily about the patron's legitimacy; al-Mutanabbī's odes are at least as much about the poet's. Does the model bend to that? Her own answer, I think, is that the poet's claims are part of what is exchanged. Find the passage. #TODO

## al-Qāḍī al-Jurjānī, *al-Wasāṭa* (excerpt, wk 9 handout)

Read 10-28 for the seminar.

- the frame: every poet errs; judge by proportion. A defense that admits faults is more persuasive than a panegyric of the poet.
- on sariqa: borrowing a predecessor's meaning is legitimate if the borrower adds to it or puts it better; theft if he only takes it. [connects to the idea that al-Mutanabbī "owns" the tradition]
- useful for the paper only for one point: medieval readers already argued about al-Mutanabbī's self-praise as a fault of excess or a quality. Possibly one footnote.
