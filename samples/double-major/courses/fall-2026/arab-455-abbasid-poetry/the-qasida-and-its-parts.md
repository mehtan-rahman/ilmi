## the unit: bayt, miṣrāʿ, qāfiya

The qaṣīda is a polythematic ode in one meter and one rhyme from first line to last. Usually 30–100 lines, some longer. The line, **bayt** (بَيْت, pl. أَبْيَات abyāt, literally "tent"), is two hemistichs; each hemistich is a **miṣrāʿ** (مِصْرَاع, "leaf of a double door"). The first half is the ṣadr (صَدْر, "chest"), the second the ʿajuz (عَجُز, "rump"). Prof. Haddad likes the anatomy: the line is a body, front and back.

the two halves of a bayt are the {{c1::ṣadr (صَدْر)}} and the {{c2::ʿajuz (عَجُز)}}; each half is a {{c3::miṣrāʿ}} ^ab-sadr-ajuz

Rhyme, **qāfiya** (قَافِيَة), is carried on one consonant, the **rawī** (رَوِيّ), which closes every line of the poem. Odes get named by it: a lāmiyya rhymes in lām, a mīmiyya in mīm, a bāʾiyya in bāʾ. The vowel after the rawī is usually long in pause (the iṭlāq), so a lāmiyya ending in -li is really ringing on -lī every line. The Imruʾ al-Qays muʿallaqa is a lāmiyya; al-Mutanabbī's "al-ḫaylu wa-l-laylu" poem is a mīmiyya.

The opening line usually rhymes in both hemistichs, so the first ṣadr also ends on the rawī: **taṣrīʿ** (تَصْرِيع). Function: announces the rhyme before the poem settles into rhyming only at line-end. Ibn Rašīq discusses it in *al-ʿUmda*; Prof. Haddad said the critics treat an opening without it as a sign of haste [find the chapter, I wrote it down without a page].

taṣrīʿ :: the opening line of a qaṣīda rhyming in both hemistichs, so the rhyme is announced in the first ṣadr ^ab-tasri
<!-- sr due=2026-11-03 st=review s=20.5 d=5 r=5 l=1 ivl=20 last=2026-10-14T19:12:00Z g=3 -->

The line is the unit of sense. Enjambment (taḍmīn) is a fault in classical criticism: a bayt should be complete in itself. This matters for later: the critics' habit of praising and blaming single lines ("the best line the Arabs said on X") comes straight out of this. Prof. Haddad: "the anthologist's knife is already in the poem's grammar." The cost: for a long time scholarship read the qaṣīda as a string of beads with no unity. Everything since the 1970s has been arguing against that.

## the canonical opening

Imruʾ al-Qays (d. mid-6th c. CE), first line of his muʿallaqa, which everyone learns first:

> قِفَا نَبْكِ مِنْ ذِكْرَى حَبِيبٍ وَمَنْزِلِ / بِسِقْطِ اللِّوَى بَيْنَ الدَّخُولِ فَحَوْمَلِ

My translation: "Halt, you two, and let us weep at the memory of a beloved and a campsite / at the sand-dune's edge, between al-Daḫūl and Ḥawmal."

Points from the session:

- qifā is a dual imperative: the poet addresses two companions, a convention so fixed that later poets keep the dual with nobody there. Ibn Qutayba knew it was odd; some grammarians explained it as an emphatic singular (qif qif → qifā). Prof. Haddad: take the dual at face value, the poem needs witnesses.
- nabki is jussive after the imperative (in answer to the command): "so that we may weep".
- ḏikrā ḥabībin wa-manzili: the beloved and the place are one memory. The place-names are real places and also not; nobody reading in Baghdad in 850 knew where al-Daḫūl was. That is the point: the names are relics.
- meter ṭawīl (see the prosody tab; this line is the textbook ṭawīl example, with qabḍ in the last foot of each half).

## the three movements

The scheme we are working with (it is a scheme, and Prof. Haddad wants us to keep saying so):

1. **nasīb** (نَسِيب): the amatory-elegiac prelude. The poet halts at the ruins, remembers the beloved, weeps.
2. **raḥīl** (رَحِيل): the desert journey on the she-camel, away from the ruins.
3. the **ġaraḍ** (غَرَض, "aim"): boast (faḫr) of self and tribe in the pre-Islamic ode; praise of a patron (madīḥ) in the court ode; also satire (hijāʾ), elegy, wisdom (ḥikma) closing.

Not every ode has all three. Many jāhilī odes have nasīb + faḫr; many ʿAbbāsid panegyrics have nasīb + madīḥ with the raḥīl reduced to a line or two, or gone.

### nasīb

Stock motifs, which we listed on the board:

- **aṭlāl** (أَطْلَال, sg. ṭalal): the traces of the abandoned camp. Hearth-stones (aṯāfī), the trench dug round the tent against rain (nuʾy), tent-peg holes, dung of the gazelles now living there. Rain and wind have effaced and re-exposed them.
- **wuqūf**: the halt itself. The poet stops and questions the traces, which do not answer.
- **bukāʾ**: weeping; the companions tell him to be patient (ṣabr), he cannot.
- **ẓaʿn** (ظَعْن) / ẓaʿāʾin: the departing women in their camel-litters, seen receding, often in an extended description.
- **ṭayf al-ḫayāl**: the phantom of the beloved visiting at night (much more ʿAbbāsid than jāhilī in frequency; Abū Tammām and al-Buḥturī love it).

the traces of the abandoned campsite that open the nasīb are the {{c1::aṭlāl (أَطْلَال)}} ^ab-atlal

What the nasīb does, three readings from the session:

1. *Ibn Qutayba's (functional) reading*: it wins the hearer's attention because love is universal (below).
2. *Elegiac*: the nasīb is about loss of the past as such; the beloved is a figure for time. J. Stetkevych (*The Zephyrs of Najd*) on nostalgia for Najd as a lost place that becomes a poetic place. [I have not read this, only Prof. Haddad's summary. Is it worth it for the paper? Probably not.]
3. *Ritual*: Suzanne Stetkevych's rite-of-passage model (below). The nasīb is separation.

Prof. Haddad's own line, which I wrote in caps: "the nasīb is not a preface. It is the poem's first argument: that the past cannot be held. Everything after is a reply."

### raḥīl

The she-camel (nāqa), described part by part: her hump, her flanks, her pace, her endurance, compared in extended similes to an onager, a wild cow (oryx) hunted by dogs, an ostrich. These similes become small narratives of their own (the onager and his mates going to water, the oryx sheltering from rain under a tree at night then facing the hunters' dogs at dawn). Seminar debate: are these digressions? Labīd's oryx episode is twenty lines. Kamila (comp lit) argued they are the poem's real content and the madīḥ is the frame; Yusuf said that is a modern taste for the descriptive; Prof. Haddad: both, and the poets themselves were aware of it, see how the ʿAbbāsids shrink it.

The raḥīl ends with arrival, and the camel is often worn out, sacrificed, or left behind. In the court ode the arrival is at the patron's door.

The transition into the ġaraḍ is the **taḫalluṣ** (تَخَلُّص, "getting free"). The critics prize a smooth one (ḥusn al-taḫalluṣ). The jāhilī ode often just jumps; the ʿAbbāsid ode makes the turn a point of craft, often within one line.

taḫalluṣ :: the transition from the prelude (nasīb or raḥīl) into the main aim of the poem, e.g. into the madīḥ; a point of craft judged by the critics ^ab-takhallus

### ġaraḍ: faḫr and madīḥ

In the tribal ode the third part is faḫr: the tribe's generosity, horses, raids, the poet's own drinking and gambling (maysir) and hospitality. In the court ode: madīḥ of the patron, praising the same virtues transferred to a ruler: generosity (karam, jūd), courage, lineage, piety, justice, the ruler as rain-cloud. The madīḥ often closes with a petition, implicit or open, and sometimes with a short ḥikma passage.

The virtues praised in madīḥ are the old tribal ones (generosity, valor, protection of the weak), so the court ode says that the caliph is the tribe's ideal man, made a state. Prof. Haddad: "the madīḥ is a contract stated as a description."

## Ibn Qutayba's account

Ibn Qutayba (d. 276/889), introduction to *al-Šiʿr wa-l-šuʿarāʾ* ("Poetry and Poets"). He reports from an unnamed "man of learning" (baʿḍ ahl al-adab) the reason for the order of the parts. My paraphrase of the handout translation:

The composer of qaṣīdas began with the abodes and traces, wept, complained, and addressed the companion to halt, so as to have occasion to mention the departed inhabitants. He then joined this to the nasīb, complaining of love's pain and longing, so as to incline the hearts of the listeners and draw their faces toward him, since love is close to every soul. When he knew he had their attention, he followed with the claim of what is owed: he described the journey, the fatigue, the night travel, the wasting of the camel. When he had established the patron's obligation to him (that he had suffered to come), he began the madīḥ, urging the patron to reward him and placing him above his peers.

Discussion:

- It is an ʿAbbāsid reading of jāhilī poetry, made from inside a court culture. Ibn Qutayba reads the old odes as if they were panegyrics with petitions. Most of the muʿallaqāt have no patron at all.
- It is normative. He goes on to say that the "excellent poet" keeps the proportion and does not overextend any part, and that the later poet should not stop at a ruin of mud-brick where the ancients stopped at a campsite, or ride a mule where they rode a camel. So it is a defense of the old model against the moderns (with Abū Nuwās hovering, see that tab).
- But it is not stupid: it names the exchange. The poet suffers (raḥīl) and so has a claim (ḥaqq). Ritual-exchange readings of the madīḥ are, Prof. Haddad says, Ibn Qutayba with anthropology added.

## the ritual reading (S. Stetkevych)

Suzanne Pinckney Stetkevych, *The Mute Immortals Speak* (1993), reads the tripartite ode on van Gennep's rite of passage:

- nasīb = separation (from the beloved, the past, the tribe's former place)
- raḥīl = liminality (the desert, danger, the self stripped, the sacrificed camel)
- faḫr / madīḥ = aggregation (reintegration into the tribe, or into the court with a new status)

S. Stetkevych reads the qaṣīda's parts as van Gennep's rite of passage: nasīb = {{c1::separation}}, raḥīl = {{c2::liminality}}, madīḥ/faḫr = {{c3::aggregation}} ^ab-rite-passage

Her later work (*The Poetics of Islamic Legitimacy*, 2002) extends this to the court: the panegyric as a ritual exchange in Mauss's sense. The poet offers the ode, the patron is obliged to return a gift (the jāʾiza, prize), and the exchange affirms the patron's legitimacy while it binds him. The poem is the gift; it is also the record that the gift was given, which can be withdrawn by satire if the return fails. Hence the danger of the disappointed panegyrist.

This is the frame for my paper (see term paper tab). The Kāfūr poems are what happens when the exchange fails and the poet takes the gift back.

Objections from the seminar, so I have them:

- Kamila: the model is so flexible it fits anything with three parts. Prof. Haddad: fair, but the test is whether it explains *details*, e.g. why the camel is sacrificed, why the poet must suffer before praising. It does.
- Yusuf: medieval critics never talk like this. Prof. Haddad: medieval critics never talk about the unity of the ode at all, which is not evidence that it lacks one.
- Me (I said something, for once): the ritual reading works best when the poet and patron both want the exchange to succeed; it does not tell you what to do when the poet is performing the exchange while not believing in it. Prof. Haddad: "write that down." So I did, and it is more or less my paper.

## from tribe to court: what changes by 750

Rough list made on the board at the end of wk 2, to be filled in over the term:

| feature | jāhilī ode | early ʿAbbāsid court ode |
| --- | --- | --- |
| audience | tribe, rival tribes, the fair (sūq) | patron, court, other poets competing |
| ġaraḍ | faḫr, sometimes madīḥ of a chief | madīḥ, with petition |
| raḥīl | long, the camel and the animal tableaux | short, often a single line or two, or dropped |
| nasīb | aṭlāl, departing women | aṭlāl kept as convention, mocked, or replaced (wine, garden, the city) |
| transmission | rāwī (transmitter), memory | written dīwān, compiled, commented on |
| criticism | anecdote, verdicts at fairs | philologists, then theorists of badīʿ |

the term for the ode's "aim", the main section after the prelude (faḫr, madīḥ, hijāʾ …) is {{c1::ġaraḍ (غَرَض)}} ^ab-gharad

The muʿallaqāt as a group: seven (or ten in some lists) famous long pre-Islamic odes, collected in the ʿAbbāsid period (the compilation tradition points to Ḥammād al-Rāwiya, 8th c.). The story that they were hung (muʿallaqa = "suspended") on the Kaʿba is late and probably etiology. Prof. Haddad: "the hanging is a metaphor for canon. The ʿAbbāsids hung them."
