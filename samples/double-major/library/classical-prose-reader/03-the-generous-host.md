## Passage 3. The Generous Host (الكَرِيمُ وَالضَّيْفُ)

Level: Part I, late. New constructions: the circumstantial clause with *wa-* and *qad*; the oath with *wa-llāhi* and the negated imperfect; verbs of beginning; the counterfactual with *law* … *la-*. Read the passage aloud twice before turning to the notes.

حُكِيَ أَنَّ رَجُلًا مِنْ أَهْلِ البَادِيَةِ كَانَ مَعْرُوفًا بِالكَرَمِ، لَا يَنْزِلُ بِهِ ضَيْفٌ إِلَّا أَكْرَمَهُ، وَإِنْ لَمْ يَكُنْ عِنْدَهُ إِلَّا القَلِيلُ. فَنَزَلَ بِهِ ذَاتَ لَيْلَةٍ رَكْبٌ مِنَ المُسَافِرِينَ، وَقَدْ أَصَابَتِ النَّاسَ سَنَةٌ شَدِيدَةٌ، فَلَمْ يَبْقَ فِي بَيْتِهِ شَيْءٌ يُطْعِمُهُمْ إِيَّاهُ.

فَقَالَتْ لَهُ امْرَأَتُهُ: «مَا تَصْنَعُ بِهِمْ وَلَيْسَ عِنْدَنَا إِلَّا هٰذِهِ النَّاقَةُ الَّتِي نَعِيشُ مِنْ لَبَنِهَا؟» فَقَالَ: «وَاللّٰهِ لَا يَبِيتُ ضَيْفِي جَائِعًا وَأَنَا حَيٌّ.» ثُمَّ قَامَ إِلَى النَّاقَةِ فَنَحَرَهَا، وَأَوْقَدَ النَّارَ، وَجَعَلَ يَشْوِي اللَّحْمَ وَيُقَدِّمُهُ إِلَيْهِمْ بِيَدِهِ حَتَّى شَبِعُوا.

فَلَمَّا أَصْبَحُوا قَالَ لَهُ كَبِيرُهُمْ: «أَتَعْرِفُ مَنْ نَزَلَ بِكَ؟» قَالَ: «لَا، وَمَا سَأَلْتُكُمْ، فَإِنَّ الضَّيْفَ لَا يُسْأَلُ عَنِ اسْمِهِ حَتَّى تَمْضِيَ ثَلَاثٌ.» فَقَالَ: «أَنَا عَامِلُ الخَلِيفَةِ عَلَى هٰذِهِ النَّاحِيَةِ، وَقَدْ خَرَجْتُ مُتَنَكِّرًا لِأَعْرِفَ أَحْوَالَ النَّاسِ.»

ثُمَّ أَمَرَ لَهُ بِمِائَةِ نَاقَةٍ وَقَالَ: «هٰذِهِ مَكَانَ نَاقَتِكَ، وَلَوْ كَانَ عِنْدِي أَكْثَرُ مِنْهَا لَأَعْطَيْتُكَ إِيَّاهُ.» فَقَالَ الرَّجُلُ: «إِنَّمَا أَطْعَمْتُكُمْ لِوَجْهِ اللّٰهِ، لَا لِأَنَالَ بِهِ شَيْئًا.» فَأَبَى العَامِلُ إِلَّا أَنْ يَقْبَلَهَا، فَقَبِلَهَا وَفَرَّقَ أَكْثَرَهَا عَلَى جِيرَانِهِ. فَصَارَ يُضْرَبُ بِهِ المَثَلُ فِي البَادِيَةِ، فَيُقَالُ: «أَكْرَمُ مِنْ صَاحِبِ النَّاقَةِ.»

## Glossary

- حُكِيَ *ḥukiya* — it is related (passive of حَكَى *ḥakā*, to tell, narrate)
- البَادِيَة *al-bādiya* — the desert, the steppe; the bedouin as a way of life (b-d-w)
- نَزَلَ بِـ *nazala bi-* — to alight at, stop as a guest with
- رَكْب *rakb* — a party of riders (collective; cf. رَاكِب rider)
- سَنَة *sana* — here a year of drought or famine, as often in old prose
- لَبَن *laban* — milk (in classical usage fresh milk, not yoghurt)
- نَحَرَ *naḥara* — to slaughter (a camel) by a stab at the base of the throat (النَّحْر)
- أَوْقَدَ *awqada* — to kindle (IV of w-q-d)
- شَوَى، يَشْوِي *šawā, yašwī* — to roast
- شَبِعَ *šabiʿa* — to eat one's fill
- كَبِير *kabīr* — here the leader, the senior member of a group
- عَامِل *ʿāmil* — agent; governor of a district on behalf of the caliph
- مُتَنَكِّر *mutanakkir* — disguised (V participle of n-k-r)
- مَكَانَ *makāna* — in place of, instead of (adverbial accusative)
- نَالَ، يَنَالُ *nāla, yanālu* — to obtain
- أَبَى، يَأْبَى *abā, yaʾbā* — to refuse

## Notes on grammar

**The narrative opening.** حُكِيَ أَنَّ "it is related that" is one of a family of impersonal openings: حُكِيَ, رُوِيَ, قِيلَ, ذُكِرَ, and in Kalīla wa-Dimna زَعَمُوا أَنَّ. The passive conceals the transmitter; in the anthologies proper the anecdote would usually carry a chain of transmitters instead (حَدَّثَنَا فُلَانٌ قَالَ…). After أَنَّ the subject is accusative: رَجُلًا, and its predicate here is a whole verbal sentence, كَانَ مَعْرُوفًا بِالكَرَمِ.

**Exception after negation.** لَا يَنْزِلُ بِهِ ضَيْفٌ إِلَّا أَكْرَمَهُ, literally "no guest alights with him but he honours him." When إِلَّا follows a negated clause and is itself followed by a verb, the construction does not except a noun at all; it restricts the whole statement: the only thing that ever happens when a guest arrives is that he is honoured. The perfect أَكْرَمَ after إِلَّا is idiomatic here and carries no past meaning; it states what invariably follows. Translate freely: "no guest ever stopped with him without his treating him generously."

**Concessive *wa-in*.** وَإِنْ لَمْ يَكُنْ عِنْدَهُ إِلَّا القَلِيلُ "even if he had nothing but a little." The conditional particle إِنْ preceded by وَ often has concessive force, "even if." Note that يَكُنْ is jussive after لَمْ, with the long vowel shortened (يَكُونُ → يَكُنْ). The noun of كَانَ, القَلِيلُ, is nominative and placed after its predicate عِنْدَهُ, which is a prepositional/adverbial phrase; this order is normal when the predicate is an adverb or prepositional phrase and the noun is restricted by إِلَّا.

**Adverbial accusative of time.** ذَاتَ لَيْلَةٍ "one night." The noun ذَات, here meaning roughly "a certain one of," stands in the accusative as a *ẓarf zamān* (adverb of time) and governs لَيْلَةٍ in the genitive by *iḍāfa*. Compare ذَاتَ يَوْمٍ "one day," which you will meet in nearly every passage of Part II.

**The circumstantial clause with *wa-qad*.** وَقَدْ أَصَابَتِ النَّاسَ سَنَةٌ شَدِيدَةٌ "a severe year of drought having struck the people." This is a *jumlat ḥāl*: a clause, introduced by the so-called *wāw al-ḥāl*, describing the circumstances in which the main action takes place. With a perfect verb the clause is normally introduced by وَقَدْ, and the time reference is anterior: the drought had already come when the riders arrived. Notice also the word order. The object النَّاسَ comes before the subject سَنَةٌ; Arabic allows this freely, since the case endings show who does what, and here it keeps the long subject phrase (noun plus adjective) at the end of the clause. The verb is feminine (أَصَابَتْ) because the subject سَنَة is feminine, even though it comes last; the kasra on أَصَابَتِ is a helping vowel before the *hamzat al-waṣl* of النَّاس.

**The indefinite antecedent.** شَيْءٌ يُطْعِمُهُمْ إِيَّاهُ "a thing that he might feed them" (literally: a thing he would feed them it). An indefinite noun is described by a following sentence without any relative pronoun (*jumla ṣifa*). The resumptive pronoun is required: here it is the independent object pronoun إِيَّاهُ, used because the verb already carries one pronoun object (هُمْ) and the second object cannot be suffixed in ordinary prose. أَطْعَمَ takes two objects: the person fed and the food.

**A second *ḥāl* clause, this time nominal.** مَا تَصْنَعُ بِهِمْ وَلَيْسَ عِنْدَنَا إِلَّا هٰذِهِ النَّاقَةُ "what will you do with them, when we have nothing but this she-camel?" The clause after وَ is a nominal sentence negated by لَيْسَ. Again the noun of the kāna-verb, النَّاقَةُ, follows its fronted predicate عِنْدَنَا and is nominative.

**The oath.** وَاللّٰهِ لَا يَبِيتُ ضَيْفِي جَائِعًا "By God, my guest will not spend the night hungry." The oath particle وَ (*wāw al-qasam*) puts the name of God in the genitive. In the answer to an oath (*jawāb al-qasam*) a negative future is commonly expressed with لَا and the imperfect, as here; a positive future in the same position takes لَـ and the energetic (لَأَفْعَلَنَّ), which you will meet in Passage 9. The verb بَاتَ "to spend the night" is one of the sisters of كَانَ and could be analysed with جَائِعًا as its predicate; the editors prefer to treat بَاتَ here as a full verb and جَائِعًا as a single-word *ḥāl*, "spend the night (in the state of being) hungry." Either analysis gives accusative جَائِعًا.

**The *ḥāl* clause without a verb.** وَأَنَا حَيٌّ "while I am alive." A nominal clause with its own subject pronoun, introduced by the *wāw al-ḥāl*. This is the commonest way to say "while X is Y" in classical prose. Contrast the single-word *ḥāl* جَائِعًا in the same sentence: one describes the guest, the other the speaker.

**Verbs of beginning.** جَعَلَ يَشْوِي اللَّحْمَ "he set about roasting the meat." The verbs جَعَلَ, أَخَذَ, طَفِقَ, and أَنْشَأَ, when followed directly by an imperfect without أَنْ, mean "to begin to," and the imperfect describes the ongoing action. They are counted among the *afʿāl al-šurūʿ*, a subclass of the sisters of كَادَ. The second verb, يُقَدِّمُهُ, is coordinated with يَشْوِي and depends on the same جَعَلَ.

**The continuative *ḥattā*.** حَتَّى شَبِعُوا "until they had eaten their fill." With a perfect verb حَتَّى simply marks the end point of a past action. With a subjunctive (حَتَّى تَمْضِيَ, below) it marks a goal or a limit not yet reached.

**The relative/interrogative *man*.** أَتَعْرِفُ مَنْ نَزَلَ بِكَ "do you know who stopped with you?" مَنْ may be read as an interrogative ("do you know: who stopped…?") or as a relative ("do you know the one who…"). In indirect questions the two readings coincide in meaning.

**The answer and its reason.** وَمَا سَأَلْتُكُمْ، فَإِنَّ الضَّيْفَ لَا يُسْأَلُ… "and I did not ask you, for a guest is not asked…" The perfect negated by مَا is the usual negative of a past action in narrative. The فَ before إِنَّ introduces the explanation. حَتَّى تَمْضِيَ ثَلَاثٌ "until three (nights) have passed": the counted noun لَيَالٍ is understood, and since لَيْلَة is feminine the numeral appears in its masculine-looking form without *tāʾ marbūṭa*, following the reverse agreement of the numbers three to ten. The verb is feminine, agreeing with the implied nights.

**Purpose with *li-*.** لِأَعْرِفَ أَحْوَالَ النَّاسِ "in order to learn the condition of the people." The *lām al-taʿlīl* takes the subjunctive. The same construction appears negated in لَا لِأَنَالَ بِهِ شَيْئًا "not so as to obtain anything by it."

**Counterfactual *law*.** وَلَوْ كَانَ عِنْدِي أَكْثَرُ مِنْهَا لَأَعْطَيْتُكَ إِيَّاهُ "had I had more than that, I would have given it to you." Both clauses of an unreal condition are in the perfect; the main clause is normally marked by لَـ. The independent pronoun إِيَّاهُ again carries the second object of أَعْطَى. Its masculine gender refers to the implied "more" (أَكْثَرُ), not to the camels.

**Restriction with *innamā*.** إِنَّمَا أَطْعَمْتُكُمْ لِوَجْهِ اللّٰهِ "I fed you only for God's sake." إِنَّمَا restricts the statement to what follows it, often to its last element: the point is the motive, لِوَجْهِ اللّٰهِ, not the feeding.

**Refusing all but one thing.** فَأَبَى العَامِلُ إِلَّا أَنْ يَقْبَلَهَا "the governor refused (everything) except that he accept them," that is, insisted that he accept them. أَبَى is felt as a negative verb, so it can be followed by an exception with إِلَّا, exactly as a negated verb can. This is a fixed and frequent idiom.

**The proverb.** أَكْرَمُ مِنْ صَاحِبِ النَّاقَةِ "more generous than the owner of the she-camel." Comparative proverbs of the form أَفْعَلُ مِنْ فُلَانٍ are a genre in themselves: أَكْرَمُ مِنْ حَاتِمٍ, أَذْكَى مِنْ إِيَاسٍ. The collections of proverbs list hundreds of them, each with the anecdote that is supposed to explain it, and passages like this one imitate that form. The elative أَكْرَمُ is diptote and does not take *tanwīn*.

## Cultural notes

**Hospitality (*al-karam*, *al-ḍiyāfa*).** Generosity to the guest is the central virtue of the pre-Islamic and early Islamic bedouin ethic, and much adab literature exists to praise it. Its emblematic figure is Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī, a poet of the sixth century whose name became proverbial. A recurring motif, found also in this passage, is the host who slaughters his only riding or milch camel for a guest: the generosity is measured by what it costs the giver, and a camel in a year of drought is very nearly the household's life.

**The three days.** The host's refusal to ask the guest's name reflects a widely cited norm that a guest is entitled to three days of hospitality without being asked his business. The point of not asking is that the host's generosity should not depend on who the guest is.

**The disguised official.** The visitor who turns out to be a caliph, vizier, or governor travelling in disguise is a stock figure of the adab anecdote, familiar also from the stories of Hārūn al-Rašīd in the *Thousand and One Nights*. He serves to test a virtue and then to reward it, and the reward is usually out of all proportion to the sacrifice: here a hundred camels for one.

**The ʿāmil.** In the early Abbasid administration the ʿāmil was the caliph's agent in a district, responsible for taxes and order. The editors use the word loosely here; in a historical text you would want to know whether a ʿāmil, a wālī, or an amīr is meant.
