## Passage 11. The Judge and the Two Litigants (القَاضِي وَالخَصْمَانِ)

Level: Part II, opening. New constructions: the dual in narrative; *aḥaduhumā* and *al-āḫar*; verbs with two objects; the chain of imperatives with *fa-*; *laʿalla*; *baʿdu* cut off from its complement; the vocative of a construct phrase; the passive of verbs of mental state. The anecdote belongs to a large family of stories about clever judges, of which the hero is often named as Iyās ibn Muʿāwiya, qāḍī of Basra under the Umayyads; the editors leave the judge anonymous.

اِخْتَصَمَ رَجُلَانِ إِلَى قَاضٍ مِنْ قُضَاةِ البَصْرَةِ فِي كِيسٍ فِيهِ أَلْفُ دِرْهَمٍ. فَقَالَ أَحَدُهُمَا: «أَيُّهَا القَاضِي، أَوْدَعْتُ هٰذَا الرَّجُلَ أَلْفَ دِرْهَمٍ قَبْلَ سَفَرِي، فَلَمَّا رَجَعْتُ جَحَدَنِي.» وَقَالَ الآخَرُ: «مَا أَوْدَعَنِي شَيْئًا، وَلَا رَأَيْتُ لَهُ دِرْهَمًا قَطُّ.»

فَقَالَ القَاضِي لِلْأَوَّلِ: «أَلَكَ بَيِّنَةٌ؟» قَالَ: «لَا، وَإِنَّمَا دَفَعْتُهَا إِلَيْهِ وَلَيْسَ مَعَنَا أَحَدٌ.» قَالَ: «فَأَيْنَ دَفَعْتَهَا إِلَيْهِ؟» قَالَ: «تَحْتَ شَجَرَةٍ فِي طَرَفِ المَدِينَةِ.» قَالَ القَاضِي: «فَانْطَلِقْ إِلَى تِلْكَ الشَّجَرَةِ فَخُذْ مِنْهَا وَرَقَاتٍ وَأْتِنِي بِهَا، لَعَلَّ اللّٰهَ يَجْعَلُ لَكَ فِيهَا بَيِّنَةً.»

فَمَضَى الرَّجُلُ، وَجَلَسَ الآخَرُ بَيْنَ يَدَيِ القَاضِي سَاكِتًا. فَأَقْبَلَ القَاضِي عَلَى غَيْرِهِ مِنَ الخُصُومِ يَنْظُرُ فِي أُمُورِهِمْ، حَتَّى إِذَا مَضَتْ سَاعَةٌ الْتَفَتَ إِلَيْهِ فَقَالَ لَهُ، كَأَنَّهُ يَسْأَلُ عَنْ أَمْرٍ هَيِّنٍ: «أَتُرَاهُ بَلَغَ الشَّجَرَةَ بَعْدُ؟» قَالَ: «لَا، إِنَّهَا بَعِيدَةٌ مِنْ هُنَا.»

فَقَالَ القَاضِي: «يَا عَدُوَّ اللّٰهِ، تَزْعُمُ أَنَّكَ لَمْ تَأْخُذْ مِنْهُ شَيْئًا، وَأَنْتَ تَعْرِفُ الشَّجَرَةَ وَمَكَانَهَا!» فَبُهِتَ الرَّجُلُ وَلَمْ يُحِرْ جَوَابًا، ثُمَّ أَقَرَّ بِالمَالِ. فَلَمَّا رَجَعَ صَاحِبُهُ بِالوَرَقَاتِ قَالَ لَهُ القَاضِي: «قَدْ جَاءَتْكَ بَيِّنَتُكَ قَبْلَ أَنْ تَأْتِيَ بِهَا»، وَأَمَرَ بِرَدِّ الأَلْفِ إِلَيْهِ، وَحَبَسَ الجَاحِدَ حَتَّى تَابَ.

## Glossary

## Notes on grammar

**The dual and its pronouns.** رَجُلَانِ "two men" is the nominative dual (genitive and accusative رَجُلَيْنِ). Once the pair is introduced the narrator distinguishes them as أَحَدُهُمَا "one of them" and الآخَرُ "the other." The pronoun ـهُمَا is the dual suffix, used for both genders. Note that the verb before a dual subject stands in the singular: in اِخْتَصَمَ رَجُلَانِ the verb precedes and does not agree in number. Had the subject come first, the verb would have been dual: الرَّجُلَانِ اخْتَصَمَا.

**The vocative with *ayyuhā*.** أَيُّهَا القَاضِي "O judge." A noun with the article cannot follow يَا directly; the particle أَيُّهَا (fem. أَيَّتُهَا) intervenes, and the noun after it is nominative. In speech to a judge or a ruler the vocative is usually the first word of a plaint.

**Verbs with two objects.** أَوْدَعْتُ هٰذَا الرَّجُلَ أَلْفَ دِرْهَمٍ "I deposited with this man a thousand dirhams." Many Form IV verbs are causatives of Form I and take two objects, the first being the person and the second the thing: أَعْطَى، أَطْعَمَ، أَلْبَسَ، أَوْدَعَ. In مَا أَوْدَعَنِي شَيْئًا the first object is the suffix ـنِي, the second شَيْئًا. The *nūn al-wiqāya* in ـنِي protects the final vowel of the verb.

**Numerals and the counted noun.** أَلْفُ دِرْهَمٍ "a thousand dirhams." مِائَة and أَلْف take the counted noun in the genitive singular. Contrast passage 3, بِمِائَةِ نَاقَةٍ.

**The temporal *lammā*.** فَلَمَّا رَجَعْتُ جَحَدَنِي "and when I returned, he denied me." لَمَّا followed by a perfect means "when, after"; its main clause is also perfect, and is often (not here) introduced by فَـ or إِذَا.

**Never, with *qaṭṭu*.** وَلَا رَأَيْتُ لَهُ دِرْهَمًا قَطُّ "and I have never seen a dirham of his." قَطُّ is indeclinable (*mabnī* on ḍamma) and is used only with a negated perfect, to deny that something ever happened. With the future the corresponding word is أَبَدًا. The second negative here is لَا, not مَا, because it coordinates with the preceding مَا: وَلَا "nor."

**The interrogative *hamza* with a fronted predicate.** أَلَكَ بَيِّنَةٌ "do you have proof?" The nominal sentence لَكَ بَيِّنَةٌ, with its prepositional predicate first, is made a question by the prefix أَ. The answer لَا is followed by an explanation introduced by وَإِنَّمَا "rather, it was only that…."

**Another *ḥāl* clause.** دَفَعْتُهَا إِلَيْهِ وَلَيْسَ مَعَنَا أَحَدٌ "I handed them over to him when there was no one with us." Compare passage 3. The pronoun ـهَا in دَفَعْتُهَا refers to the dirhams; non-human plurals take feminine singular agreement.

**The chain of imperatives.** فَانْطَلِقْ إِلَى تِلْكَ الشَّجَرَةِ فَخُذْ مِنْهَا وَرَقَاتٍ وَأْتِنِي بِهَا "go to that tree, take some leaves from it, and bring them to me." The imperatives are joined by فَـ (sequence) and وَ (accompaniment). Two points of form deserve attention. First, خُذْ is the irregular imperative of أَخَذَ, which drops its initial *hamza* (likewise كُلْ from أَكَلَ, مُرْ from أَمَرَ). Second, the imperative of أَتَى "to come" is ائْتِ, with a *hamzat al-waṣl* and the radical *hamza* resting on a *yāʾ* seat. When it is preceded by وَ or فَ the connective *hamza* falls away in pronunciation and in spelling, and the radical *hamza* moves to an *alif* seat: وَأْتِ, *waʾti*. With the suffix of the first person this gives وَأْتِنِي, *waʾtinī*, "and bring me." The same spelling is found in the Qurʾān, وَأْتُوا البُيُوتَ مِنْ أَبْوَابِهَا. The verb أَتَى بِـ means "to bring," literally "to come with."
<!-- extract note-on-watini -->

**The hope with *laʿalla*.** لَعَلَّ اللّٰهَ يَجْعَلُ لَكَ فِيهَا بَيِّنَةً "perhaps God will make proof for you in them." لَعَلَّ is a sister of إِنَّ: its subject is accusative (اللّٰهَ), and its predicate here is a verbal sentence. The judge's words are, of course, a pretext, and the reader should hear their irony.

**Sitting "between the hands."** بَيْنَ يَدَيِ القَاضِي "before the judge," literally "between the two hands of the judge." The dual يَدَانِ in construct loses its *nūn*: يَدَا (nom.), يَدَيْ (gen./acc.), here genitive after بَيْنَ. The final *yāʾ* takes a helping kasra before the article: يَدَيِ القَاضِي.

**The single-word *ḥāl*.** جَلَسَ … سَاكِتًا "he sat silent." The *ḥāl* is an indefinite accusative describing the state of a definite noun, here الآخَرُ, at the time of the action.

**The verbal *ḥāl*.** فَأَقْبَلَ القَاضِي عَلَى غَيْرِهِ مِنَ الخُصُومِ يَنْظُرُ فِي أُمُورِهِمْ "the judge turned to other litigants, looking into their affairs." An imperfect verb can stand directly as a circumstantial clause without *wāw*: يَنْظُرُ describes what the judge was doing while the time passed.

**The narrative *ḥattā idhā*.** حَتَّى إِذَا مَضَتْ سَاعَةٌ الْتَفَتَ إِلَيْهِ "until, when an hour had passed, he turned to him." This is a fixed narrative pattern: حَتَّى إِذَا + perfect, followed by a main clause in the perfect without فَ. It marks the moment towards which the preceding action was leading. سَاعَة here is "a while," not a sixty-minute hour.

**The *kaʾanna* clause.** كَأَنَّهُ يَسْأَلُ عَنْ أَمْرٍ هَيِّنٍ "as though he were asking about a trivial matter." كَأَنَّ is a sister of إِنَّ; its subject is the suffix ـهُ. Placed between قَالَ لَهُ and the quotation, it describes the manner of speaking.

**The passive of supposing.** أَتُرَاهُ بَلَغَ الشَّجَرَةَ بَعْدُ "do you suppose he has reached the tree yet?" تُرَى is the passive of تَرَى, and in this idiom it means "you are made to think, you suppose." It is very common in questions. Its first object is the suffix ـهُ; the clause بَلَغَ الشَّجَرَةَ stands in the place of a second object.

**The word *baʿdu*.** بَعْدُ "yet, still." When بَعْد loses its complement (بَعْدَ ذٰلِكَ "after that") and the complement is understood, it becomes indeclinable and takes ḍamma: بَعْدُ. The same applies to قَبْلُ "before(hand)." In a question or a negative, بَعْدُ means "yet": لَمْ يَأْتِ بَعْدُ "he has not come yet."

**The vocative of a construct phrase.** يَا عَدُوَّ اللّٰهِ "O enemy of God!" A noun in construct after يَا is accusative (*munādā muḍāf*). Compare يَا عَبْدَ اللّٰهِ.

**Claiming.** تَزْعُمُ أَنَّكَ لَمْ تَأْخُذْ مِنْهُ شَيْئًا "you claim you took nothing from him." زَعَمَ is followed by أَنَّ and marks the claim as the speaker's report, often a disbelieved one. Here the following *ḥāl* clause, وَأَنْتَ تَعْرِفُ الشَّجَرَةَ وَمَكَانَهَا, exposes it.

**The passive form *buhita*.** فَبُهِتَ الرَّجُلُ "the man was dumbfounded." Several verbs of mental state are found mostly or only in the passive form: بُهِتَ, جُنَّ "to go mad," غُشِيَ عَلَيْهِ "to faint." They are translated as active intransitives in English.

**The jussive of a hollow Form IV.** لَمْ يُحِرْ جَوَابًا "he returned no answer." The verb is أَحَارَ، يُحِيرُ (IV of ḥ-w-r); after لَمْ the jussive يُحِرْ shortens the long vowel before the final unvowelled consonant, as يَكُنْ from يَكُونُ.

**Feminine agreement and fronted object.** قَدْ جَاءَتْكَ بَيِّنَتُكَ "your proof has come to you already." The verb is feminine because بَيِّنَة is, and the object suffix ـكَ attaches to the verb before the subject. The judge's remark plays on أَتَى بِـ: the proof came before the plaintiff could "come with" it.

**Verbal nouns after *amara bi-*.** أَمَرَ بِرَدِّ الأَلْفِ إِلَيْهِ "he ordered that the thousand be returned to him." The verbal noun رَدّ (from رَدَّ, doubled) stands in for a clause, and its object الأَلْف is in the genitive by *iḍāfa*. This construction is far commoner in classical prose than أَمَرَ أَنْ يُرَدَّ….

## Cultural notes

**The qāḍī.** The judge in early Islamic society was appointed by the ruler and decided cases on the basis of the religious law as it was coming to be understood. He held court in the mosque or in his house, and the scene of litigants waiting their turn while the judge hears other disputes is a realistic one.

**Proof and oath.** A maxim of Islamic procedure, often quoted, runs: البَيِّنَةُ عَلَى مَنِ ادَّعَى، وَاليَمِينُ عَلَى مَنْ أَنْكَرَ "proof is upon the claimant, and the oath upon the one who denies." The plaintiff here has no witnesses, and in the normal course the defendant would have cleared himself by an oath. The story is satisfying because the judge finds a third way.

**The deposit.** A *wadīʿa* is property entrusted to someone for safekeeping, and betraying a deposit was regarded as one of the gravest breaches of trust. Merchants who travelled commonly left their cash with a trusted person, and the denial of a deposit is a frequent theme of adab and of legal handbooks.

**The proverbial judge.** The comparative أَذْكَى مِنْ إِيَاسٍ "more astute than Iyās" belongs to the same family of proverbs as passage 3's أَكْرَمُ مِنْ… . Stories of Iyās's acuity form a small genre of their own, and the trick of making a defendant betray knowledge he claims not to have is among the commonest.
