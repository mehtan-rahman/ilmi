## Passage 15. The Merchant Who Was Robbed (التَّاجِرُ وَاللُّصُوصُ)

Level: Part II. New constructions: first-person narration within a frame; *baʿḍ* in the sense "a certain"; the description of an indefinite noun by a negated clause; *a-mā* and *balā*; the dual relative; *lā yazālu*; *fa-mā … ḥattā*. The passage belongs to the literature of "relief after hardship" (*al-faraj baʿd al-šidda*) in a loose sense, though its relief is moral rather than miraculous.

حَدَّثَ بَعْضُ التُّجَّارِ قَالَ: خَرَجْتُ مِنَ البَصْرَةِ أُرِيدُ الشَّامَ فِي قَافِلَةٍ عَظِيمَةٍ، وَمَعِي مَالٌ كَثِيرٌ جَمَعْتُهُ فِي عِشْرِينَ سَنَةً. فَلَمَّا كُنَّا فِي بَعْضِ الطَّرِيقِ خَرَجَ عَلَيْنَا قَوْمٌ مِنَ اللُّصُوصِ، فَأَخَذُوا مَا مَعَنَا، وَلَمْ يَتْرُكُوا لِأَحَدٍ مِنَّا إِلَّا ثَوْبَهُ الَّذِي عَلَيْهِ.

فَجَعَلَ التُّجَّارُ يَبْكُونَ وَيَلْطِمُونَ وُجُوهَهُمْ، وَكَانَ فِينَا شَيْخٌ لَمْ يَتَكَلَّمْ بِكَلِمَةٍ، بَلْ جَلَسَ عَلَى حَجَرٍ يَنْظُرُ إِلَى اللُّصُوصِ مُبْتَسِمًا. فَعَجِبْتُ مِنْهُ وَقُلْتُ لَهُ: «يَا شَيْخُ، أَمَا ذَهَبَ مَالُكَ كَمَا ذَهَبَتْ أَمْوَالُنَا؟» قَالَ: «بَلَى.» قُلْتُ: «فَمَا يُضْحِكُكَ؟»

قَالَ: «يَا ابْنَ أَخِي، إِنِّي خَرَجْتُ مِنْ بَيْتِي وَمَا أَمْلِكُ شَيْئًا، فَكَسَبْتُ هٰذَا المَالَ بِالأَسْفَارِ، ثُمَّ ذَهَبَ كَمَا جَاءَ. وَقَدْ عَلِمْتُ أَنَّ المَالَ عَارِيَّةٌ، وَأَنَّ العَارِيَّةَ مَرْدُودَةٌ. وَبَقِيَ لِي مَا لَا يَأْخُذُهُ لِصٌّ.» قُلْتُ: «وَمَا هُوَ؟» قَالَ: «عَقْلِي الَّذِي كَسَبْتُ بِهِ المَالَ أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ، وَيَدَايَ اللَّتَانِ عَمِلْتُ بِهِمَا.»

فَمَا مَضَتْ سَنَةٌ حَتَّى رَأَيْتُهُ فِي سُوقِ دِمَشْقَ وَقَدْ صَارَ لَهُ حَانُوتٌ عَظِيمٌ، وَأَنَا لَا أَزَالُ أَبْكِي عَلَى مَا ذَهَبَ مِنِّي. فَعَلِمْتُ أَنَّ الغِنَى غِنَى النَّفْسِ، وَأَنَّ مَنْ ذَهَبَ صَبْرُهُ ذَهَبَ مَالُهُ مَرَّتَيْنِ.

## Glossary

- حَدَّثَ *ḥaddaṯa* — to relate, tell (II of ḥ-d-ṯ); the verb of oral transmission
- تَاجِر، ج تُجَّار *tājir, tujjār* — merchant
- الشَّام *al-Šām* — Syria, the Levant (also Damascus)
- قَافِلَة، ج قَوَافِل *qāfila, qawāfil* — caravan
- خَرَجَ عَلَى *ḫaraja ʿalā* — to come out against, attack; to rebel against
- لِصّ، ج لُصُوص *liṣṣ, luṣūṣ* — thief, robber
- ثَوْب *ṯawb* — garment
- لَطَمَ *laṭama* — to slap, strike (the face, in mourning)
- شَيْخ *šayḫ* — old man
- حَجَر *ḥajar* — stone
- مُبْتَسِم *mubtasim* — smiling (VIII participle of b-s-m)
- عَجِبَ مِنْ *ʿajiba min* — to wonder at, be astonished by
- أَضْحَكَ *aḍḥaka* — to make laugh (IV of ḍ-ḥ-k)
- مَلَكَ، يَمْلِكُ *malaka, yamliku* — to own
- كَسَبَ *kasaba* — to earn, acquire
- عَارِيَّة *ʿāriyya* — a loan of an object, to be returned (also عَارِيَة)
- مَرْدُود *mardūd* — returned; to be given back
- حَانُوت، ج حَوَانِيت *ḥānūt, ḥawānīt* — shop
- غِنًى *ġinan* — wealth; independence of need
- صَبْر *ṣabr* — patience, endurance

## Notes on grammar

**The frame.** حَدَّثَ بَعْضُ التُّجَّارِ قَالَ "one of the merchants related, saying." The verb قَالَ introduces the narrator's own words, and the rest of the passage is in his first person. This doubling (*ḥaddaṯa … qāla*) is the standard frame of a transmitted report, and is preserved even when, as here, there is no chain of names. بَعْض in construct with a definite plural means "one of, a certain": بَعْضُ التُّجَّارِ "a certain merchant." Later in the passage فِي بَعْضِ الطَّرِيقِ means "somewhere along the road," literally "in part of the road."

**Purpose as circumstance, again.** خَرَجْتُ مِنَ البَصْرَةِ أُرِيدُ الشَّامَ "I left Basra making for Syria." The verb أَرَادَ with a place as object means "to head for." Compare passage 12.

**A clause on an indefinite noun.** مَالٌ كَثِيرٌ جَمَعْتُهُ فِي عِشْرِينَ سَنَةً "much money that I had gathered over twenty years." The noun مَال, indefinite, is described first by the adjective كَثِيرٌ and then by a clause, with the resumptive ـهُ. The numeral عِشْرِينَ takes its counted noun in the accusative singular (*tamyīz*): سَنَةً.

**Nominal *ḥāl* with a prepositional predicate.** وَمَعِي مَالٌ كَثِيرٌ "with much money on me." The clause is nominal, its predicate مَعِي fronted because its subject is indefinite.

**The sense of *kāna* in narrative.** فَلَمَّا كُنَّا فِي بَعْضِ الطَّرِيقِ "when we were somewhere along the road." كَانَ here has a prepositional phrase as its predicate and simply places the narrators in time.

**Restriction of the object.** وَلَمْ يَتْرُكُوا لِأَحَدٍ مِنَّا إِلَّا ثَوْبَهُ الَّذِي عَلَيْهِ "and left none of us anything but the garment he had on." Since the negative clause lacks an object, the noun after إِلَّا takes the case the verb would give it: ثَوْبَهُ, accusative. This is the same "emptied" exception (*istiṯnāʾ mufarraġ*) as in passage 3, where the noun after إِلَّا was nominative because it was the subject.

**Verbs of beginning with a plural subject.** فَجَعَلَ التُّجَّارُ يَبْكُونَ وَيَلْطِمُونَ وُجُوهَهُمْ "the merchants began to weep and beat their faces." The verb جَعَلَ is singular before its plural subject, but the imperfects that follow agree with that subject in full: يَبْكُونَ, يَلْطِمُونَ. The subject has been named, and the verbs after it must agree.

**The indefinite noun described by a negated clause.** شَيْخٌ لَمْ يَتَكَلَّمْ بِكَلِمَةٍ "an old man who did not say a word." As above, the clause after the indefinite noun is its description; it may be negative like any other clause. بِكَلِمَةٍ "a (single) word" is an object with بِـ, a usage common with تَكَلَّمَ.

**Correction with *bal*.** بَلْ جَلَسَ عَلَى حَجَرٍ "rather, he sat on a stone." After a negative statement بَلْ introduces what was in fact the case.

**Two kinds of *ḥāl* together.** يَنْظُرُ إِلَى اللُّصُوصِ مُبْتَسِمًا "looking at the robbers, smiling." The imperfect يَنْظُرُ is a verbal *ḥāl* describing the old man (as in passages 11 and 12); مُبْتَسِمًا is a single-word *ḥāl* describing him while he looks. Two *ḥāl*s on one noun are unremarkable.

**The vocative of an indefinite-looking noun.** يَا شَيْخُ "old man!" A single noun addressed directly after يَا (*munādā mufrad ʿalam* or *nakira maqṣūda*) takes ḍamma without *tanwīn*. Contrast يَا عَدُوَّ اللّٰهِ (passage 11), where the noun is in construct and therefore accusative.

**Negative question and its answer.** أَمَا ذَهَبَ مَالُكَ "didn't your money go?" The interrogative أَ before the negative مَا asks a question that expects agreement. The answer بَلَى "yes (it did)" contradicts the negative: it affirms the positive proposition. نَعَمْ in reply to a negative question would confirm the negative ("yes, it didn't"). The distinction is classical and strict.

**What makes you laugh?** فَمَا يُضْحِكُكَ "then what makes you laugh?" The Form IV causative أَضْحَكَ "to make laugh" has the old man as its object. The narrator interprets the smile as laughter.

**The vocative with *ibn*.** يَا ابْنَ أَخِي "nephew," literally "son of my brother," a kindly address from an older man to a younger. ابْن is in construct, so accusative. Its *hamzat al-waṣl* is written but not pronounced: *yā-bna aḫī*.

**A *ḥāl* clause negated with *mā*.** خَرَجْتُ مِنْ بَيْتِي وَمَا أَمْلِكُ شَيْئًا "I left my house owning nothing." Note the *wāw*: a negated imperfect ḥāl clause may take it (passage 11's extract note: the affirmative normally does not).

**Coordination of *anna* clauses.** وَقَدْ عَلِمْتُ أَنَّ المَالَ عَارِيَّةٌ، وَأَنَّ العَارِيَّةَ مَرْدُودَةٌ "I have come to know that wealth is a loan, and that a loan is to be returned." The chain is a little syllogism; the second clause takes up the predicate of the first as its subject. The words echo a saying widely quoted in the moral literature.

**The relative *mā* with a negated clause.** وَبَقِيَ لِي مَا لَا يَأْخُذُهُ لِصٌّ "and what remains to me is what no thief takes." The verb بَقِيَ precedes its subject, which is the relative clause مَا لَا يَأْخُذُهُ لِصٌّ. The resumptive ـهُ refers to مَا.

**The dual relative and the dual suffix.** وَيَدَايَ اللَّتَانِ عَمِلْتُ بِهِمَا "and my two hands with which I worked." يَدَايَ is the nominative dual يَدَانِ with the first-person suffix: the *nūn* of the dual drops in construct, and the suffix ـيَ is written with fatḥa. يَد is feminine, so the dual relative is اللَّتَانِ (nominative; genitive and accusative اللَّتَيْنِ). The resumptive pronoun in the relative clause is dual: بِهِمَا.

**The frame *fa-mā … ḥattā*.** فَمَا مَضَتْ سَنَةٌ حَتَّى رَأَيْتُهُ "a year had not passed before I saw him," i.e. "within a year I saw him." The negated perfect followed by حَتَّى and a perfect is an idiom for "no sooner … than" or "not long before."

**Two *ḥāl* clauses after *raʾaytuhu*.** وَقَدْ صَارَ لَهُ حَانُوتٌ عَظِيمٌ "and he had come to have a great shop": the *wāw* + *qad* + perfect of passage 3, with صَارَ, a sister of كَانَ, whose predicate لَهُ precedes its noun حَانُوتٌ. Then وَأَنَا لَا أَزَالُ أَبْكِي "while I was still weeping": a nominal clause whose predicate is itself a sentence with لَا أَزَالُ. The verb زَالَ, used only negated (مَا زَالَ, لَا يَزَالُ), is a sister of كَانَ meaning "still to be, not to cease." Its predicate here is the imperfect أَبْكِي.

**Conditional with *man*.** مَنْ ذَهَبَ صَبْرُهُ ذَهَبَ مَالُهُ مَرَّتَيْنِ "whoever loses his patience loses his money twice." مَنْ as a conditional pronoun takes a perfect (or jussive) in both clauses, and the perfects have general present meaning. The dual مَرَّتَيْنِ is an adverbial accusative: "twice."

## Cultural notes

**Basra and the Syrian route.** Basra, at the head of the Gulf, was the great port of the Abbasid east, and caravans from it to Syria crossed the desert by routes that were notoriously exposed to raiding. The robbers here are a commonplace of travel narratives; the merchants' response, slapping their faces, is a conventional gesture of grief.

**Wealth as a loan.** The idea that worldly goods are on loan, to be reclaimed by their true owner, is a moral commonplace with roots in both pre-Islamic wisdom and Islamic piety. The old man's last sentence connects it to a value the passage has not named but everywhere implies, *ṣabr*, steadfast patience.

**The closing maxims.** The first, أَنَّ الغِنَى غِنَى النَّفْسِ "true wealth is the wealth of the soul," echoes a well-known saying attributed to the Prophet, which the reader will meet in its full form in Part III. The second, on losing one's money twice, appears to be the editors' own coinage in the proverbial manner.
