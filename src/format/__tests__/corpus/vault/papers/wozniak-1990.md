## §1 — the premise

Memory is not a vessel that fills with study, nor a muscle that strengthens with reps. It is closer to a forest: each path you walk gets a little easier to walk again, and the ones you stop walking close over. What we call learning is the discipline of choosing which paths to walk.

Choosing well requires honesty about how memory behaves. The classical curve falls fast and flattens slow. Each successful recall pushes the curve out further than the last. A scheduler’s job is to surface a recall just before the curve crosses the floor — not earlier, not later.

## §2 — twenty rules of formulating knowledge

The twenty rules are the residue of a long argument with one’s own forgetfulness. They group into four concerns: atomicity, integration, language, and process.

Atomicity is the unglamorous one and the most important. The minimum information principle — the smallest piece of knowledge you can recall standalone — is not a stylistic preference. It is the only formulation that produces a memory you can retrieve, because retrieval cues degrade with each additional concept the card asks you to track.

## §3 — the card you keep failing

Some cards you get wrong again and again. The system can flag them; only the learner can repair them. The repair is almost never on the card itself — it is one level up, in the passage that produced it.

## §4 — on cramming

Cramming is mass repetition compressed in time. It solves the problem of an exam tomorrow morning and nothing that lasts. The forgetting curve is largely insensitive to how many times you ran the deck on the eve — what matters is the spacing between successful recalls, not the count of them.
